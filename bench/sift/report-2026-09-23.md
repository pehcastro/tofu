Cost unit: money. A money figure is dollars that left the account behind the credential named above, read from the response of the call it names and never from a rate card.

**No model call and no Jev call was made to write this report.** Every measured figure is read from `.tofu/log`, read only. The test is `TestTheShellSiftPointHasBeenMeasured` in this package and its output is pasted below whole.

## The answer

**`shell_sift` is live. The ledger carries 13 real `shell_sift` decisions, all from one turn, and this report measures them instead of guessing from TOFU-217.** `library/tools/shell/rules/shell_sift@1.yaml` now reads `mode: enforced`, so TOFU-434's flip is the reason these 13 rows exist at all. Thirteen decisions is not a rate: it is one turn's worth of evidence, and the section below says exactly what it cannot answer.

## The ledger count

**13 shell_sift rows**, counted directly with `grep -c shell_sift .tofu/log/*.jsonl` and again through `ReadLedger`, which agrees: 3,039 total rows across 9 files, 13 of them `shell_sift`. ## Milliseconds and dollars, from the 13 rows

**p50 361 ms, p95 524 ms, worst 722 ms. p50 $0.0000331, p95 $0.0000337, worst $0.0000339. $0.000431 spent in total across all 13.** Every number here is read off the `latency_ms` and `cost` fields the wire response wrote into the ledger row, not computed from a rate.

TOFU-217 predicted $0.0000303 per decision from its 594-decision, two-run log. The 13 measured rows land at $0.0000331 median, **9 percent above the prediction.** The prediction was close. It is still retired in favor of the measurement: the derivation lives on here as the comparison, not as the report's own number.

TOFU-217's 838 ms figure was never a per-decision number, it was an aggregate added to one shell tool call under concurrency 4. The 13 measured rows are per-decision, so they cannot be compared to 838 ms on the same units without the batching arithmetic the test still does: p95 measured 524 ms times a synthetic 8.7-candidate depth at concurrency 4 lands at 1,572 ms, well above the 838 ms TOFU-217 arm. **That comparison borrows a candidate count from the 34-row test corpus, not from the two real calls, because only two real calls exist and one of them carried 12 decisions and the other 1.** State the batching estimate for what it is, a corpus-shaped guess dressed in a real p95.

## What changed the shell result, against what did not

**9 of 13 decisions cut the chunk, still_needed under the shipped 0.50 threshold, 4 left it whole.** Read from `keep_at: 0.5` in `library/tools/shell/rules/shell_sift@1.yaml` through `sift.LoadShellRule`, the same threshold enforcement uses, against the `still_needed` noul on each of the 13 recorded answers. Nine cutting is a majority, four not cutting is a real minority: this is not a rule that always fires.

## What thirteen samples cannot answer

**The 13 decisions are not 13 independent shell calls.** They come from exactly two shell tool calls, both inside one turn, `turn-18d7e7074db17e6c`, both inside one recorded session: a `cat` of five files that split into 14 chunks, 12 judged and 2 held, and a `bun install` that split into 3, 1 judged and 2 held. Every millisecond and dollar figure above describes that one turn, one task, one command shape. It says nothing about:

- how the point behaves on a different command, a different tool, a different shell exit code
- session-to-session spread, since n is 1 session, not the 110 the byte and session-count figures below still draw on
- a real tail: p95 and worst sit 361 ms apart on 13 points, and a single slow call anywhere in a wider sample could move the p95 more than the whole spread seen so far
- whether the 9-of-13 cut rate holds outside this one task's shell output, which reads as ordinary source and config files, not the error-shaped or novel content the point exists to catch

Thirteen calls, here is what they cost, and it is not enough to decide whether the point behaves the same way on the next hundred.
